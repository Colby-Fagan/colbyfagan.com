The monitoring gap in AI biosecurity

Colby Fagan · November 2026

Superhuman biology expertise may soon become widely accessible through AI, yet we lack the means to robustly understand and prevent misuse. Frontier labs, gene synthesis companies, and aviation offer precedents for a solution.

1. Safeguarding against superhuman biological capabilities

AI now outperforms human experts on biosecurity-relevant benchmarks [1]. Previously, if a model performed as well as an 80th-percentile virologist on a test of hands-on virology knowledge [2], that expert’s laboratory capabilities offered a rough reference point for interpreting the model’s score. Now that many models score above all the experts tested [3], that comparison has been lost. Benchmark performance alone gives us limited insight into what these models can help someone accomplish in practice. Safeguard design therefore needs evidence beyond benchmark scores, including evidence of how people actually attempt to misuse models.

Direct tests of biological misuse capabilities could themselves generate biological or informational hazards [3, 32]. Instead, researchers must track nearby proxies: narrow biology tasks, AI uplift in defensive biology (biology, but not misuse), and AI uplift in criminal activities (misuse, but not biology) [3]. The most concrete evidence, however, is actual attempts at biological misuse in the real world. Real misuse attempts can help us identify what safeguards need to address. Studying these attempts can reveal the tasks users seek help with, the assistance models provide, and the weaknesses users exploit in existing protections. These observations inform us which risks to evaluate and which safeguards to strengthen.

Anthropic's September 2026 report shows Claude users already doing risky biology work [4], but Anthropic sees only its own platform. Meanwhile, a single open-weight inference provider, Fireworks, says it now processes more than 40 trillion tokens a day [5] – more, they claim, than OpenAI and Gemini APIs combined [6]. Many such providers don't retain transcript data unless customers opt in [7, 8] and the open-weight models that these providers serve have far weaker safeguards, making them even more attractive for malicious users. Taken together, this means: a) bio-misuse is likely already occurring on open-weight platforms, and b) much of this activity goes unmonitored.

Misuse attempts show how AI is leveraged for dangerous biology, but we see only a sliver of them, and the critical window to learn from them is closing. Today's models (probably) aren’t capable enough to help attackers build weapons of mass destruction, so failed attempts on these surfaces go unnoticed, while each gain in capability makes the next attempt more likely to succeed. Without monitoring at open-weight inference providers, we leave that data on the table. We must not make successful harm the prerequisite for learning about attempted misuse, because the first instance of successful superhuman-level bioterrorism could be the last.

2. Misuse is moving to unmonitored surfaces

The combination of fast-advancing open-weight capability with weaker-and-removable safeguards and zero data retention defaults is pushing the riskiest activity onto surfaces with the least oversight.

Open-weight models only lag behind the frontier in AI biological capabilities by about 8 months, according to metrics from SecureBio [9]. While the capability gap is only 8 months [9], the safeguard gap is much larger. Kimi K3, the best-performing open-weight model on SecureBio's Bio Capability Index, refuses only 26.9% of the prompts SecureBio says models should refuse for general users, while the most capable closed models refuse over 90% [9]. Unless open-weight release trends change, today's superhuman biology capabilities will appear in open weights in only a matter of months, without the strong refusals of closed models.

Safeguards on open-weight models are cheap to remove. Even if current or future open-weight models refuse key requests in the course of a misuse attempt, it costs tens of dollars to fine-tune away their refusal behavior. In a September report, RAND reliably stripped refusal training from frontier open-weight models this way [10]. Abliteration, a technique that edits refusal behavior directly out of a model’s weights, works too: in one case, an abliterated MiniMax-M2.7 complied with 99% of harmful requests, up from 0% for the original model [10]. The main caveat of both approaches is that they often decrease the model’s capabilities along with its refusals. However, with over 11,000 models tagged “abliterated” on Hugging Face as of October 2026 [11], attackers can take their pick of safeguard-tampered open-weight models.

Closed-model refusal safeguards push attackers to less monitored surfaces. Refusal safeguards tell the model when to refuse a risky request [12]. When a refusal is triggered, the attacker doesn’t receive the desired information, but they also get an immediate signal to switch models or modify their elicitation strategy. By contrast, monitoring can collect information on an attacker’s interactions with a model without immediately blocking the activity. Because platforms with stronger monitoring also tend to enforce stricter refusals, those best positioned to detect and study misuse attempts drive attackers toward platforms where their activity is less visible. In the September 2026 report, Anthropic describes a platform used by overseas researchers to obtain covert access to Claude [4]. In one case, the platform was used by a researcher pursuing dangerous gain-of-function research on the chikungunya virus. After Anthropic’s first attempt at revoking the platform’s Claude access, it began routing prompts to more permissive competing models [4]. Although it’s unconfirmed whether these “competing models” were closed or open-weight, the mechanism here is clear: when confronted with strong safeguards, users are pushed to more permissive, and presumably less monitored, platforms.

Monitoring at inference providers is necessary, but not sufficient. The easiest way to access powerful AI models, whether closed or open-weight, is to access them through an API. However, users also have the option to run models locally, which makes monitoring more difficult. Better approaches, such as elevated customer diligence and in-silicon monitoring [13], are needed to solve the self-hosting issue. Despite this, accessing a model through an API is still the easiest method for an attacker, and the data that we collect from misuse on the platforms that we do monitor will help inform safeguards elsewhere.

3. Inference providers can't see most misuse today

Three of the largest providers serving open-weight models, by daily token volume as of mid-2026, are: Fireworks AI (~40 trillion tokens/day), Together AI (~13 trillion tokens/day), and OpenRouter (~3.6 trillion tokens/day) [14–16].* These platforms tend to prioritize user privacy over monitorability:

  • Fireworks AI "does not log or store prompt or generation data for any open models, without explicit user opt-in" [7]
  • OpenRouter says prompts are "not retained unless you specifically opt in to prompt logging" [8]
  • Together AI retains prompt data by default, but offers zero data retention (ZDR) as an option [17].

Relying solely on built-in model safeguards is not sufficient to protect against misuse, even if it may be preferable for user privacy. Frontier labs have realized this; as of June 2026, Anthropic requires 30-day retention for traffic on Mythos-class models† to protect against "attacks that operate across many requests" [18], despite its acknowledgment that this decision "will be unpopular with customers who have come to expect zero retention” [19]. However, there are promising paths toward multi-turn monitoring solutions that preserve user privacy. OpenAI’s Private Safety Processing, as one example, emits a narrow safety signal based on multiple interactions over time, which staff can review without reading the actual content of the conversation, which is compatible with ZDR [20]. Systems like this can be used to preserve ZDR and keep employee eyes off of user conversations unless a critical threshold of evidence of misuse is met.

4. Monitoring is feasible

Monitoring for bio-misuse is feasible, and it doesn’t require blocking users or having staff read every conversation. A scanner can review logs after the fact, look for warning signs that build up across many turns and sessions, and flag the most concerning conversations for a human to review first. The tools to build and run this efficiently already exist.

Companies like Anthropic and OpenAI use systems for semi-automated misuse detection [21–23], proving that tools like this are technically feasible. The problem is a lack of ready-made monitoring solutions available outside of these frontier platforms – tools that inference providers can quickly and easily adopt. This is the issue that my team seeks to solve with ASTRAL [24], a bio-specific misuse scanner for AI transcripts we've built over the last few months. The goal is to help inference providers cut through the volume and flag cases of probable bio-misuse, so they can take appropriate action. This kind of monitoring creates the opportunity for anonymized information sharing to inform the creation of robust safeguards across the industry.

5. We need a system for reporting misuse incidents

The next critical step is to build a way to share bio-misuse incident data. If inference providers don't share findings with safeguard developers, law enforcement, and policymakers, we lose most of the benefit of monitoring. That benefit comes from aggregating incident data, detecting patterns across platforms, and using those patterns to develop technical safeguards, industry standards, and policy solutions that make every platform safer, not just the ones that detected the incidents.

We are likely in a critical window for misuse-data aggregation. If current models still can’t help most attackers create biological weapons, then today's failed attempts tell us which safeguards need to be in place before models are capable enough for such attempts to succeed. If we fail to monitor these attempts and understand current misuse patterns, we are underinformed when designing solutions, and consequently vulnerable to future catastrophic attacks. Fortunately, there are precedents here, so we don’t need to start from scratch.

Aviation built a near-miss reporting system after a preventable crash. In 1974, a United Airlines crew made a dangerous error on the approach into Washington Dulles, and United warned only its own pilots. Weeks later, TWA Flight 514 made the same error on the same approach and crashed, killing everyone aboard [25]. The FAA responded with the Aviation Safety Reporting System (ASRS), which NASA runs as a neutral go-between so pilots, controllers, and mechanics can report near-misses without going to their regulator [26]. Reporting is voluntary, confidential, and comes with limited immunity from FAA penalties, and NASA strips identifying details [25, 27]. ASRS now receives about 10,000 reports a month and has issued over 8,150 safety alerts to the organizations in a position to fix the problems [27]. Failed misuse attempts are AI’s near-misses, and aviation suggests that a trusted intermediary and limited protections encourage reporting.

California’s SB 53 is the only frontier AI incident-reporting law currently in force in the US, but it does not capture bio-misuse near-misses. Since January 1, 2026, it has required frontier developers to report critical safety incidents to the state’s Office of Emergency Services within 15 days [28]. The law has two gaps for bio-misuse. First, it applies only to the handful of companies that train frontier models, so an inference provider serving open-weight traffic has no obligation to report. Second, a biological incident only counts once a catastrophic risk has materialized [29]. A failed attempt doesn’t qualify, so SB 53 makes successful harm the prerequisite for reporting. Future AI models may enable the engineering of catastrophic novel pathogens [33]; therefore, we cannot wait for a successful attack to begin incident reporting.

Biotechnology shows that an industry can build the missing early-warning layer itself. In 2009, five gene synthesis companies formed the International Gene Synthesis Consortium and agreed on a common protocol for screening orders and customers, before the U.S. government published draft screening guidance on November 27, 2009 [30, 31]. Members also reserved the right to refuse any order and to notify authorities about potentially problematic ones [30]. Together, those five founding companies said they represented roughly 80% of global synthesis capacity [30]. Inference providers could do the same for misuse data: agree on a common incident format, report attempts through a neutral intermediary that strips identifying details (as NASA does for pilots), and receive anonymized patterns of what is being tried across the industry.

6. Conclusion — Start monitoring while attempts still fail

Models now outperform experts on biosecurity-relevant benchmarks, but scores alone give us limited insight into what they can help someone accomplish in practice. Real misuse attempts can help inform how we proceed safely into an era of superhuman biological capabilities. Those attempts are likely already occurring at open-weight inference providers, where models trail the frontier in biological capabilities by about 8 months [9], refusals can be stripped for 10s of dollars [10], and much of the traffic is never stored [7, 8]. Today, most of these attempts likely fail and go unseen, and each one we miss is data we won't have when models become capable enough for the next attempt to succeed. Monitoring them is feasible and can preserve user privacy, and aviation shows how an industry with a regulatory body can share incidents. The next step is to deploy monitoring tools at the platforms where misuse is occurring unnoticed.

Inference providers can start now. Platforms serving open-weight models likely already host misuse attempts that no one is reviewing. They can start by reviewing the logs they already keep, after the fact and without blocking user requests. For zero-retention traffic, they can look to privacy-preserving approaches like OpenAI's Private Safety Processing, which surface a narrow safety signal without staff reading user conversations.

Researchers, funders, standards bodies, and policymakers can help by building easy-to-adopt detection tools, a shared incident format, legal protection for providers who report, and a neutral intermediary that does for AI misuse what NASA does for aviation.
